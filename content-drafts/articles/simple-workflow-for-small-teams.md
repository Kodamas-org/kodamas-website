# A simple workflow for small teams (and why it's never about the columns)

A simple workflow for small teams is a small set of agreements about how work moves: where tasks live, where documents live, who has access to what, when you meet and what you decide there. You don't need a project manager to have one. You need a few good practices, chosen together, checked regularly and renegotiated when they stop working.

Team size is not the point. I have seen three people produce a quantity of documents, credentials and half-made decisions that would make a corporate archivist cry. The question is never "are we big enough for a process?". The question is "will this still work when we are one person more?".

I'm Daniela, co-founder of Kodamas. In all my years designing web and native apps I always joined companies as a designer and ended up as the project manager, mostly because somebody had to find where things were. This article is what I learned doing that, plus how Emma and I run our own two-person company, including the parts we got wrong.

## Do small teams need processes?

Yes, but a small one, and one that the team owns. A small team doesn't need a framework with ceremonies and roles. It needs to know where things are, what has been decided, and what everyone is working on this week.

The usual argument against process goes like this: "We're small, we have to be fast, process slows us down." We don't buy it. Being fast and being chaotic are two different things, and chaos is not free. Somebody pays for it, usually the person who gets interrupted all day long because nobody can find the login for the analytics tool. (It's saved in someone's browser. Nobody knows whose.)

There is research on this cost. In a study on interrupted work, Gloria Mark and colleagues found that people compensate for interruptions by working faster, but "this comes at a price: experiencing more stress, higher frustration, time pressure and effort" ([Mark, Gudith and Klocke, CHI 2008](https://ics.uci.edu/~gmark/chi08-mark.pdf)). So yes, a team without process can look fast. It's just running on the people's stress.

And then there is scalability. A process for three people is also for the fourth person who joins in six months and asks "where is everything?", and about the founder who would like, one day, to go on holiday without bringing the laptop to the beach.

## What happens when everything lives in the founder's head

When a small team has no shared workflow, the founder becomes the workflow. Every priority, every password and every "is this done?" goes through one person, who slowly turns into a very human, very tired search engine, and stops being able to do their own job.

I want to be clear: nobody does this on purpose. It's what naturally happens when a company grows faster than its habits. I have seen it in every small company I worked for and with, and in different shapes.

### When priorities change every day

In one of my first jobs, when I was a junior, the CEO fought to introduce a version of an agile framework adapted to our team. The main reason was protecting the team from priorities and requests that kept changing, mostly coming from the founder. The framework gave everyone a stable moment to agree on what we were doing, and a polite way to say "this goes in the next round", which is a much nicer sentence than "no".

### When there's no tool, no "done" and one person holds all the keys

In another company, a project management tool simply never existed. I was a one-person team, working basically solo on scattered inputs from the founder. Project managers came and went and, without a structure around them, they could get very little done. There was no definition of done, so nothing was ever really finished. Things were "done for now", which in small company language can mean forever. There was also very little validation, which made the work quite lonely.

And there was one detail that still gives me a small shiver: only one person managed access to all the internal tools. If you have ever had that person on holiday when the payment provider asks you to log in, you know.

### When the tool exists but nobody uses it

In a third company, someone had already tried to set up ClickUp. When I joined, nobody was using it. So everyone, me included, went to the founder for every little ask. The ClickUp workspace stayed there, beautifully organised and completely empty, like a gym membership in February. The founder couldn't focus on his own work, because his day was made of our questions.

## Why processes imposed from outside don't stick

A process dies when it's designed for a generic team instead of the real one. The tool is rarely the problem.

That ClickUp setup was made by an external person who applied a standard framework. They were not working hands on in the team, so they couldn't know its needs, its weaknesses and its strengths. The result was a well-organised workspace that matched nobody's way of working, and so nobody worked in it.

I keep this story in mind because I'm now the external person. At Kodamas we work with founders as consultants, and the easiest mistake we could make is to arrive with our favourite board and paste it on every company. So we don't.

## Start from the current state, not from a template

The first step in building a simple workflow for small teams is understanding what already exists. Before suggesting any tool or any column, look at how the team works today, what works, what doesn't, and why they feel they need help now.

This is what I check first:

- **Documentation:** where documents are created, where they live, and whether anyone can find them.
- **Accesses:** who has access to which tools, who manages them, and what happens if that person disappears for two weeks (on a beach with no signal, hopefully).
- **Basic finance:** what the team pays for, monthly, and who knows it. Subscriptions have a talent for multiplying when nobody is looking.
- **Repos and technical assets:** where the code lives and who owns the accounts (ideally someone who still works with you).
- **Existing habits:** things the team already does, even informally. A Friday message in the group chat is a habit, and habits are much easier to build on than to replace.
- **The reason they reached out:** why now. The answer usually tells you which problem hurts the most, and that's where to start.

Only after that it makes sense to talk about columns. Some teams need a board by product area, others by type of work, others by client. There is no fixed set of columns that works for every team. Sorry to everyone who came here for a template.

## A simple workflow for small teams: five good practices

A small team doesn't need a PM. It needs a few good practices that everyone agrees on. In my experience they are always the same five, whatever tool you use.

### Recurring meetings with a simple agenda

Recurring meetings are the moment where the process gets used and checked. Keep them short and keep them regular, so nobody has to "schedule a meeting about it".

Emma and I meet for 30 minutes. Our weekly team meeting agenda is very simple:

1. What each of us did since the last meeting.
2. What we decide to do before the next meeting.
3. The goal of the week.

That's it. No slides, no status reports, and so far nobody has missed them. If a topic needs more time, it gets its own moment.

### Document what has been decided

A decision that is not written down will be decided again, usually by somebody who wasn't there the first time, and often differently. Now you have two decisions, and you need a meeting to choose between them. It doesn't have to be a long document: a line on the card, a note in a shared doc, a message in the right channel. What matters is that the decision lives somewhere everyone can find it.

### Put things where everyone knows they are

"Where is it?" is one of the most expensive questions a small team asks. It usually travels with its twin: "which one is the latest version?". Agree on where things live and keep it simple enough to remember.

Once, the documentation in a company I worked with was so scattered that I created a **documentation map**: one Google Sheet where everyone could find any document with one click. It had five columns: file name, which teams it was relevant for, status, a short description and the link. The rule was easy: every time you create a document, add a row and mark it as in progress or done, and check the sheet before creating something that maybe already exists.

Honestly, it was not the most sustainable thing, and it doesn't scale very well. Every team was using a different tool for their own documentation, there was no budget for a new tool, and migrating everything to one place was not doable. In those conditions a sheet with links was the best fix available, basically a phone book for documents, and it worked well enough. I tell this story because a good practice doesn't need to be elegant. It needs to fit the constraints you actually have.

### Clear, measurable, achievable goals

A goal like "improve onboarding" can't be finished, so it never is. It just stays on the board, getting a bit older every week. A goal like "rewrite the three onboarding emails by Friday" can. Small teams have little time, so goals need to be small enough to be reached and clear enough that everyone agrees when they are.

### Accesses never in one person's hands

Keep a simple list of the tools you use, who has access to each one, and how they log in. At Kodamas we have an admin Google Sheet with every tool we subscribed to and who has which 2FA enabled. It doesn't take long to set up, and it's the difference between a bad day and a very bad week.

## A Trello workflow for small teams: how two people run Kodamas

Our own setup is a Trello board, a few Google Sheets and a shared Google Workspace with a folder structure we agreed on. It's simple on purpose, and it took us a few tries to get there.

### The tools we tried first

We started with Notion. We liked it, until we hit a limit: on the free plan, a workspace with more than one member has a limit of 1,000 lifetime blocks ([Notion developer docs](https://developers.notion.com/reference/workspace-block-limits)), and for us that meant we couldn't keep adding pages.

So we moved to Linear. Linear is a great tool, but it's built for product teams, with a structure to match. You can use it in a generic way, but for two people organising a young company it was an overkill, a bit like hiring a sommelier to pour tap water.

I think this came from my professional bias. When all you have is a product team tool, everything looks like a sprint. I'm used to those tools, so I wasn't thinking simpler. Meanwhile, I was using Trello boards for my personal life stuff, and at some point I realised it could work for Emma and me as well. It did.

### What we use now

- **Trello** for the work. At the moment our columns are "Website", "Sales & marketing", "Recurring" and "Archive". They change: "Website" will go once the website is done, and "Sales & marketing" will probably become something different once we work on client projects.
- **An admin Google Sheet** with the tools we subscribed to and who has which 2FA enabled.
- **A finance sheet** with what we spend every month on subscriptions.
- **Google Workspace** for documents, in a simple folder structure we agreed on together, so nobody has to search for "final_final_v3".

If you are looking for free project management tools for small teams, this is a decent starting point. Trello's free plan works for a small team, with one limit to know: since 2024, free Trello workspaces are limited to 10 collaborators, including guests and pending invitations ([Atlassian](https://www.atlassian.com/blog/trello/new-collaborator-limits)). For most of the teams we work with, that's plenty. If you have more than ten people, congratulations: you have different problems now.

### An honest note about our example

Emma and I run Kodamas in a very chill way. There is no pressure, we use it as a learning hub, and we work on many other things at the same time. We know this is a privileged position: we can experiment in ways that a founder with one business and one runway can't. So please take our setup as an example of the method, not as something to copy.

## A simple workflow for solo founders

Solo founders need a process too, just a smaller one. When you work alone the risk is the same as in a team: everything lives in one head, and that head is yours. The same head that also has to remember to renew the domain and buy groceries.

For my personal projects I use a standard structure: one Trello column for each project, with some cards that every column has, like the links to the docs and the things I have to do for that project. When I open the board, every project has the same shape, so I always know where to look.

It's always a matter of methodology. As long as you give yourself a set of rules, you'll be fine, as long as you are also able to tell when they don't work, or don't work anymore. If you work with a freelance developer now and then, the access list and the "where things are" rule matter even more, because they are what lets someone new start without a two-hour call.

## Signs your workflow doesn't work anymore

A process stops working long before anyone says it out loud. The signals are small, practical and very recognisable, and they are worth watching more than any dashboard.

These are the ones I have seen again and again:

- **Recurring meetings get cancelled.** Not once, regularly. The meeting is still in everyone's calendar, it has just become a reminder that a meeting once existed.
- **People ask "how do I access this?"** or "should I have access to this?".
- **Onboarding becomes guesswork:** "what should I tell the new person in this role? Which accesses do I give them?"
- **Bugs get reported in DMs** instead of in the place the team agreed on, sometimes as a screenshot with no words, which is every developer's favourite genre.
- **The founder doesn't know the status of a task**, or who is working on it. Sometimes nobody is, and everyone thinks someone else is.

None of these means the team is doing something wrong. They mean the agreement doesn't fit the team anymore, and it's time to look at it again.

## How to renegotiate your process (without it sounding like criticism)

A process is an agreement, and agreements need to be renegotiated when things change. The easiest way to do it is to change the habit first, in small, then talk about it.

### Start by giving the good example

When I see one of the signals above, I don't open with a meeting about "our communication problems". Nobody has ever left that meeting communicating better. I start using the tool myself, and I ask people to communicate with me through it, in the right channel and not in DMs. Then I extend it to the people I work closest with. Only when it's already working for a few of us do I bring it up with the whole team or the founder. At that point I'm sharing something that already works for a few of us, and that is much easier to say yes to.

### Notice what people stop doing

Emma and I used to meet three times a week, for 30 minutes. After a while we noticed that one of the three meetings got cancelled often, sometimes by me, sometimes by her. When we talked about it, we realised there were two reasons: either we were too busy with other things, or we were not giving ourselves enough work to "fill" three meetings. It turns out it's hard to set the goal of the week three times a week. So now we meet twice a week.

Nobody broke a rule there. The rule was simply wrong for the moment we were in, and the cancellations were the team telling us so.

### Make everything negotiable, people first

Emma and I are always very honest with each other. We constantly check in on our mental health, and we ask ourselves if Kodamas is still relevant to us: if we want to continue, slow down, increase the pace, or stop entirely. Everything is negotiable. Our mental health comes first.

You don't need to ask your team if the company should exist every month. But the same principle works at any size: the process is there to help the people, so the people get to say when it doesn't.

## FAQ

### Do small teams really need a process?

Yes, a small one. Even three people produce a lot of documents, accesses and decisions, and without a few shared agreements everything ends up in the founder's head. You don't need a PM or a framework, just a small set of good practices that the team agrees on and reviews.

### What is a simple workflow for a small team?

It's a short list of agreements: where tasks live (for example a Trello board), where documents live, who has access to which tools, when the team meets and what gets decided there. It should be simple enough that everyone can explain it without opening a document.

### How should I set up a Trello board for a small team?

Look at how your team works first, then choose the columns. Some teams organise by product area, others by type of work or by client. Ours has "Website", "Sales & marketing", "Recurring" and "Archive", and it changes when our work changes. There is no right set of columns, only the one that fits your team now.

### Is Trello's free plan enough for a small team?

For most small teams, yes. The main limit to know is that free Trello workspaces can have up to 10 collaborators, guests and pending invitations included. If you grow beyond that, you'll need a paid plan or a different tool.

### How often should a small team have recurring meetings?

As often as you have something to decide. If you are meeting to decide when to meet, that's too often. We started with three 30-minute meetings a week and moved to two when we noticed we kept cancelling one. If meetings get cancelled regularly, that's the signal to change the rhythm.

### How often should you review your workflow?

Whenever the signals show up: cancelled meetings, people asking how to access things, bugs reported in DMs, a founder who doesn't know who is working on what. A regular moment to ask "is this still working for us?" helps, but listening to those signals matters more than the calendar.
