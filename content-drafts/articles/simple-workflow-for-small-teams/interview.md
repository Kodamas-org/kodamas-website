# Interview: simple workflow for small teams

## Agreed before the interview

- **Primary keyword:** simple workflow for small teams
- **Secondary keywords:** Trello workflow for small teams, free project management tools for small teams, weekly team meeting agenda, do small teams need processes, workflow for solo founders
- **Slug:** simple-workflow-for-small-teams
- **Angle:** Team size is not the point. A process is an agreement: it gets checked regularly and renegotiated. The smallest version that works (Trello or free tools plus a few recurring meetings) and how to keep it alive.
- **Why:** Small teams skip process because "we're only 3 people". It's not only that things get lost: it's about scalability. Even 3 people can produce a huge amount of messy docs, credentials, etc. We don't buy the argument that because you're small you have to be fast and process slows you down. Our answer: you don't need a PM, but you need a small set of good practices.
- **Relevance:** Founders and small teams after launch, juggling dev, support and marketing without a PM, who think process is something for later.
- **Learning:** The reader can set up a minimal workflow this week: a board structure, a small set of recurring meetings with simple agendas, and the habit of reviewing and renegotiating the process every few weeks (with a solo-founder version).

## Interview notes

**Rule from Daniela: never name the companies in articles.** Company names below are for context only.

### Q1. A real moment when a small team drowned in its own mess

"I have seen this in every small company I worked for and with."

- **Warda (Daniela was a junior):** the CEO fought to start using a version of an agile framework that worked for the team. The main reason was protecting the team from the constant changes in priorities and requests, mainly from the founder.
- **HappyCow:** a project management tool never existed. A one-person team worked basically solo on scattered inputs from the founder. PMs came and went and got very little done. Feeling lonely, no validation, no definition of done. Only one person managing access to the internal tools (very dangerous).
- **Work for Impact:** they tried to set up ClickUp, but when Daniela joined nobody was using it. She had to go to the founder for every little ask, like everyone in the company, so the founder couldn't focus on his own work.

### Q2. Why the ClickUp setup died

It was set up by an external person who was applying a generic framework and wasn't working hands on in the team, so didn't know its needs, weaknesses and strengths.

### Q3. How Daniela and Emma organise Kodamas (two-person team)

**Tools tried before landing on the current setup:**
- **Notion first:** they realised it had a limit on the number of pages you can create (on their plan). *To verify in research: Notion free plan limits for teams.*
- **Then Linear:** also a limit, because it's too structured, built for product teams. You can use it in a generic way, but it's overkill.
- Daniela: "I think this came from my professional bias and I wasn't thinking simpler." She was already using Trello boards for personal life stuff, and at some point realised it could work for Emma and her as well.

**Current setup:**
- **Admin Google Sheet:** tracks the tools they subscribed to and who has which 2FA enabled to access them.
- **Finance sheet:** tracks how much they spend monthly on subscriptions.
- **Google business account** (Google Workspace) for documents, in a simple folder structure they agreed on.
- **Trello:** a set of columns that change. At the moment: "Website", "Sales & marketing", "Recurring" and "Archive". They review this structure every now and then: e.g. the Website column will go once the website is done, and Sales & marketing will probably become something different once they work on projects.
- **Meetings:** they used to meet 3 times a week for 30 minutes: decide what to do before the next meeting, share what they did, and set the goal of the week.

**Important context (honesty about the case study):** Emma and Daniela work at Kodamas in a very chill way, no pressure. They want to see what happens and use it as a learning hub while they work on many other things. So their case may not apply directly to people focused on one business only.

### Q4. Why the 3 meetings a week changed, and how they decide to change the process

- They noticed they were often cancelling one meeting a week (Daniela or Emma). They realised it was because either they were too busy with other things, or they didn't give themselves enough work to "fill" 3 meetings.
- "We are always very honest with one another." They constantly check in on each other's mental health and ask themselves if the Kodamas project is still relevant to them: continue, slow down, increase the pace, or kill it entirely.
- "Everything is negotiable, our mental health comes first."

- **Current rhythm: 2 meetings a week.**
- Daniela wants this clear: "we know our approach is not always applicable because we are in a privileged position of being able to experiment."

### Q5. What she'd set up first for a typical Kodamas founder (post-launch, 3 to 5 people, no PM)

- First: not make the same error of being the outsider who applies a standard framework to any company.
- **Check the current state first:** their documentation (basic finance, accesses, docs creation, repos, etc.), whether they already have habits, what works and what doesn't.
- **Ask why they reached out** (why they feel they need us).
- Only after really understanding the current state, suggest columns. "There is no fixed set of columns that can be applied to every project/team, but there are good practices":
  - recurring meetings
  - documenting what has been decided
  - having things where everyone knows where they are
  - having clear, measurable, achievable goals
- "It's not about columns."

**Impact on the article:** the learning should not be a board template. It's a method (look at the current state first, then a small set of good practices) with columns only as examples, clearly labelled as examples.

### Q6. A practice she introduced herself

- One time documentation was so scattered that she created a **documentation map file**: everyone in the team could simply look at one file and click on a link to find what they needed.

**6b. What the documentation map looked like**
- A **Google Sheet** with columns: file name, which teams it was relevant for, status, description, link to the doc.
- The rule: add a new row every time you create a document and mark it as in progress or done. Check if something similar is already there before creating duplicates.
- Why it was needed: every team was using a different tool to store its own documentation, there was no budget for new tools, and migrating everything to one tool wasn't doable.
- Daniela's honest take: "I know it wasn't the most sustainable thing to do, not very scalable, but at the time it was needed." "All of this was before AI and it wasn't a great example of how docs should be managed, honestly."
- **Article use:** a good-enough fix inside real constraints (no budget, no migration). It fits the "small set of good practices, not the perfect system" message. Keep her honesty about its limits.

### Q7. Solo founders

- Daniela does this with her personal projects: a standard way to organise them. **One Trello column per project**, with some common cards in each (links to docs, things she has to do for it, etc.).
- "It's always a matter of methodology: as long as you give yourself a set of rules you'll be fine (as long as you are able to tell when they don't work, or don't work anymore)."

### Q8. Signals that a process doesn't work (anymore)

Recurring things Daniela has faced:
- Meetings getting cancelled (from Q4).
- People asking "how do I access this?" or "should I have access to this?"
- Onboarding questions: "what should I tell this new person in this role, which accesses should I give them?"
- Bugs reported in DMs.
- The founder not knowing the status of a task, or who is working on it.

**8b. How she brings it up without it sounding like criticism**
- She gives the good example first: starts using a tool herself and asks people to communicate with her through it (not in DMs, and in the right channel).
- Then expands to the closest people she works with.
- Then brings it up (with the wider team / founder).

## Refined after the interview

- **Learning (updated):** The reader learns a method, not a template: look at the current state first (docs, accesses, finance, repos, existing habits, why they feel they need help), then add a small set of good practices (recurring meetings, documenting decisions, everything in a place everyone knows, clear measurable achievable goals), spot the signals that a process stopped working, and renegotiate it, starting by giving the good example. Trello setups (Kodamas, solo founder) appear as examples, not as the answer.
- **To research:** Notion free plan limits for teams (to describe Daniela's experience accurately), Trello free plan limits, a solid source on shared credentials / access management risk, possibly a source on interruptions and context switching.
